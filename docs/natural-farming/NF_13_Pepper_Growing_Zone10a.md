# Growing Capsicum Peppers in Florida Zone 10a
**Climate, variety selection, planting timing, and management strategies for rare and hot peppers in South Florida.**
*For Florida growers cultivating Capsicum annuum, C. chinense, C. frutescens, and C. baccatum in the tropics.*

---

Florida's Zone 10a (average minimum temperature 30–35°F / -1.1 to 1.7°C) encompasses Miami-Dade County, the Florida Keys, and parts of Broward County. This is one of the only places in the continental United States where Capsicum chinense varieties like the Carolina Reaper, Chocolate Habanero, and rare Caribbean peppers can be grown as near-perennials. Understanding the unique climate is the key to mastering production here.

## The Two-Season Reality of South Florida

Most of the continental US grows peppers in a single summer season. South Florida has a fundamentally different calendar:

**Cool/Dry Season (November–April):** This is the primary pepper production window. Temperatures are ideal (65–90°F days), humidity is manageable, disease pressure is lower, and rainfall is sparse enough to prevent fungal problems. Plants established in October–November will fruit heavily through April.

**Hot/Wet Season (June–October):** Intense humidity, daily rainfall, and temperatures regularly exceeding 95°F create a high-disease, high-stress environment. Annual varieties often struggle or die. Mature C. chinense plants can survive and even produce, but pest and disease management becomes critical. This is when JADAM JS + JWA becomes essential.

**Transition months (May and October–November):** The planting windows. Start seeds or transplants in August–September to have plants ready for the cool-dry season.

## Variety Selection

### Best Varieties for IC SPICY Florida Production

**Capsicum chinense** (superhots, habaneros, Caribbean types):
- Thrives in Zone 10a's heat. Heat tolerance is excellent.
- High disease tolerance relative to other species in Florida conditions.
- Varieties: Carolina Reaper, Chocolate Bhutlah, Peach Ghost Scorpion, Habanero (orange, red, chocolate), Trinidad Moruga Scorpion, 7-Pot Primo.
- These are the flagship IC SPICY varieties.

**Capsicum baccatum** (aji types):
- From South American mountains — more forgiving of slightly cooler temperatures.
- Excellent flavor profile, lower heat, productive vines.
- Varieties: Aji Amarillo, Aji Lemon Drop, Aji Fantasy, Bishop's Crown.
- Good for hot sauce ingredient diversity.

**Capsicum frutescens** (bird peppers):
- Tabasco and Bird's Eye types. Extremely heat-tolerant and virtually bulletproof in Zone 10a.
- Small fruit, high production.
- Good intercrop to demonstrate year-round productivity.

**Capsicum annuum**:
- Bell peppers, jalapeños, serranos, cayennes.
- Prefer cooler temperatures — produce best in Zone 10a's cool-dry season only.
- High disease susceptibility during summer.

## Planting Calendar for Zone 10a

| Activity | Timing |
|---|---|
| Start seeds indoors | Late August – September |
| Transplant outside | October – November (after last 95°F+ days) |
| Vegetative growth | November – January |
| Heavy production | January – April |
| Summer stress period | June – September (mature plants only; annuals struggle) |
| Second-season transplant | Possible in October for C. chinense |

C. chinense and baccatum are true perennials in frost-free Zone 10a — with proper management they can produce for 3–5+ years. Hard-pruning mature plants in late April (after main season) and resetting with KNF soil preparation can rejuvenate them for the following season.

## Common Florida Pepper Problems and Natural Farming Solutions

### Blossom-End Rot (BER)

**Cause:** Calcium does not reach rapidly expanding fruit tissue. Most often caused by inconsistent watering, not soil calcium deficiency.

**KNF solution:**
- WCA foliar spray at 1:1,000, applied every 7–10 days once flowers appear
- Consistent irrigation (drip preferred) — calcium moves with water flow
- Avoid irregular wet/dry cycles; mulch heavily to retain moisture

**University of Florida IFAS note:** "Blossom-end rot is usually not caused by insufficient calcium in the soil. Rather, it results from poor calcium uptake in the fruit due to inadequate delivery to rapidly growing tissues." (EDIS SL 284)

### Anthracnose (Colletotrichum spp.)

**Cause:** Fungal infection of fruit during humid conditions. A major problem June–October in Florida. Appears as sunken dark spots on fruit.

**Natural farming solutions:**
- JADAM Sulfur (JS) at 200:1 + JWA applied preventively every 7–14 days during wet season
- Fermented seawater spray (CGNF protocol) every 10–14 days
- OHN + BRV cocktail to discourage fungal colonization
- Remove and dispose of infected fruit immediately — do not compost infected material

### Powdery Mildew

**Cause:** Fungal pathogen, common in mild temperatures and moderate humidity. Less prevalent in Zone 10a's hot summer but appears in the cool season.

**Natural farming solutions:**
- OHN + BRV cocktail spray weekly to preventively suppress
- JADAM JS at 200:1 + JWA at first sign
- Ensure good airflow — avoid dense planting

### Aphids and Thrips

**Cause:** Common year-round in Zone 10a. Thrips are vectors for Tomato Spotted Wilt Virus (TSWV).

**Natural farming solutions:**
- OHN foliar spray deters many insects
- JADAM JHS at 200:1 + JWA for heavier infestations
- Encourage beneficial insects — avoid broad-spectrum sprays that kill predators

### Phytophthora Root Rot

**Cause:** Soil-borne water mold (Oomycete) in waterlogged conditions.

**Prevention:**
- Well-draining soil — raised beds in Zone 10a's clay-heavy soils
- IMO soil building creates drainage-supporting soil structure
- Never let water pool around pepper bases
- Soil drench with LAB helps suppress water-mold pathogens

## Soil and Container Notes

**Raised beds:** In Miami-Dade County, native soil is often marl or shallow limestone with poor drainage. Raised beds with 12+ inches of amended soil are strongly recommended.

**Mix for raised beds:**
- 40% quality topsoil or compost base
- 30% aged hardwood compost
- 20% perlite or coarse horticultural sand (drainage)
- 10% biochar (optional — improves water retention and microbial colonization)
- Inoculate with IMO4 at 1 cup per cubic foot of mix

**Container growing:** C. chinense can be grown successfully in 5-gallon containers minimum; 10–15 gallons preferred for full-sized superhot plants. Use well-draining potting mix (not Florida native soil) and drip or wick irrigation.
