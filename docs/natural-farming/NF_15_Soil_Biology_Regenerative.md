# Soil Biology and Regenerative Soil Management
**Understanding the soil food web, organic matter, and regenerative practices that make KNF and JADAM work.**
*For growers who want to understand why the methods work, not just how to follow the recipes.*

---

Korean Natural Farming and JADAM both work by supporting the living ecosystem in the soil. Understanding the fundamental biology explains why these inputs are chosen, why timing matters, and how to troubleshoot problems that don't fit a recipe.

This document draws on Matt Powers' framework from *The Regenerative Grower's Guide to Garden Amendments* and *The Permaculture Student* series, along with classical soil science, to explain the principles behind natural farming methods.

## The Soil Food Web

The soil is not just dirt. A single teaspoon of healthy, biologically active soil contains:
- 1 billion bacteria (roughly 10,000 species)
- 200,000+ fungal spores and hyphal fragments
- 10,000 protozoa
- 100 nematodes
- Countless arthropods, earthworms, and macroorganisms

These organisms form a web of relationships. Most nutrients that plants need are not absorbed directly from minerals — they are released by microorganisms that consume organic matter and excrete plant-available forms. A tomato plant does not eat compost; bacteria eat compost and excrete soluble nitrogen that the tomato absorbs.

**Matt Powers explains:** "You're not feeding the plant. You're feeding the system that feeds the plant. Your fertilization program should be designed for the microbes, not the plant."

## Bacteria vs. Fungi: The Balance KNF Seeks

Different ecosystems have different dominant microorganism types:
- **Bare soil / sand:** Dominated by bacteria, low fungal activity. Nitrogen cycling but poor structure.
- **Grassland:** Balanced bacterial-to-fungal ratio (roughly 1:1). Annual crops prefer this range.
- **Forest:** Dominated by fungi (10:1 to 100:1 fungal to bacterial ratio). Trees and perennials prefer this.
- **Crops after chemical treatment:** Often sterilized — neither fungi nor bacteria dominant. Must be rebuilt.

Master Cho designed KNF to rebuild the balanced microbial ecosystem for food crop production. IMO collects organisms from exactly the right habitat (the forest floor transition zone where both bacteria and fungi are active). Compost and organic matter feed both groups.

## Organic Matter Is Not Optional

Soil organic matter (SOM) is the fuel for all soil biological processes. It:
- Feeds bacteria and fungi
- Creates water retention (1% SOM increase = 20,000 more gallons of water per acre)
- Creates cation exchange capacity (holds nutrients)
- Supports earthworm populations
- Provides structure that prevents compaction

**Target SOM for pepper production:** 4–6% by weight. Most conventional agricultural soils in Florida are 1–2%. Building SOM is the long-term project of any natural farming program.

**How to build SOM:**
- Apply IMO4 (carbon-rich amendment)
- Mulch heavily and continuously — never leave soil bare
- Compost plant residue on site — return nutrients to the soil
- Grow cover crops in off-season beds (cowpea, buckwheat, sunn hemp in Florida)
- Minimize tillage — tillage oxidizes SOM and destroys fungal networks

## Carbon-to-Nitrogen Ratio (C:N)

The microbes that decompose organic matter require both carbon (for energy) and nitrogen (for protein synthesis). The ratio of carbon to nitrogen in a material determines how fast it decomposes and whether it releases or immobilizes nitrogen:

| Material | C:N Ratio | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh fish waste (for FAA) | 5:1 | Very fast decomp, nitrogen-releasing |
| Blood meal | 4:1 | Very fast, nitrogen-releasing |
| Compost (finished) | 15–20:1 | Moderate, nutrient-neutral |
| Straw mulch | 80:1 | Slow decomp, may temporarily tie up N |
| Wood chips | 300–500:1 | Very slow, builds long-term C |
| Newspaper | 800:1 | Extremely slow decomp, structural only |

KNF inputs bridge the gap: FAA (very low C:N) provides rapid nitrogen; IMO4 (carbon-rich base material) provides structure and feeds fungi. The combination achieves balance.

## Mycorrhizal Fungi: The Plant's Hidden Root System

Mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic networks with plant roots, extending the root system by up to 1,000× in surface area. They deliver water and phosphorus to the plant in exchange for sugars. Nearly all food crops form mycorrhizal associations — the exceptions are Brassicas (cabbage family).

**Peppers are strongly mycorrhizal.** C. chinense and C. baccatum pepper varieties with established mycorrhizal networks require significantly less supplemental watering and are more resilient to drought stress than plants in sterile soil.

**How to protect and build mycorrhizal networks:**
- Do not use synthetic phosphorus fertilizers (high phosphorus signals the plant to "fire" its fungal partners)
- Minimize tillage — fungi build networks that tillage destroys
- Maintain mulch and organic matter — feeds the saprophytic fungi that share habitat with mycorrhizae
- IMO inoculation and JMS application introduce fungal organisms that may include mycorrhizal types

**Matt Powers:** "Every time you rototill, you're starting your soil biology from scratch. Every time you skip tilling and add a layer of mulch, you're banking a little more biological capital."

## Humus and Long-Term Fertility

Humus is the most stable form of organic matter — it is the dark, crumbly substance left after all easily decomposable material has been consumed. Humus is the ultimate goal of composting and mulching programs. It persists in soil for decades or centuries and is the primary driver of long-term soil fertility.

KNF's use of IMO and compost accelerates the transition from raw organic matter to humus. JADAM's simple approach (apply lots of carbon-based organic matter, inoculate with JMS) achieves the same end result at lower cost.

## Simple Soil Tests for Natural Farmers

You do not need a laboratory to assess soil health. Field assessments:

**The smell test:** Healthy soil smells like petrichor — the pleasant earthy smell after rain. This is caused by geosmin, produced by actinomycetes bacteria. No smell or chemical smell = poor biology.

**The earthworm count:** Dig a 12-inch cube of soil. Count earthworms. Target: 10+ per cubic foot. Zero earthworms = problem.

**The jar settle test:** 1 cup soil + 1 cup water in a jar. Shake vigorously. Let settle for 24 hours. Layers from bottom: gravel/sand, silt, clay, organic matter (floating). Assess texture and organic content visually.

**The penetrometer (wire flag) test:** Push a wire flag into moist soil. Healthy soil allows 12+ inches of penetration by hand. Compacted soil resists at 3–6 inches. Compaction = poor root growth and drainage.

**The brix test:** Juice a leaf from your plant and measure with a refractometer. High brix (8–12+) indicates a well-nourished plant. Low brix (3–5) indicates nutrient deficiency or poor uptake. Regular brix testing tracks your program's effectiveness.
