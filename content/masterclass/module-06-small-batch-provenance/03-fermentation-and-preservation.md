# 6.3 Fermentation & Preservation

**Outcome:** You can explain lacto-fermentation as hiring sour guardians for the jar, keep garden KNF ferments mentally separate from food ferments, and treat home-canning / shelf-stability decisions as non-negotiable public food-safety rules — not craft improvisation.

---

## Core

Lacto-ferment is hiring sour guardians for the jar.

You pack sound peppers (Habanero and Scotch Bonnet earn their keep here), salt, and time. Lactic acid bacteria — wild on vegetables or from a trusted starter — eat sugars and excrete acid. The jar's neighborhood gets too sour for many spoilage organisms. Your job is hospitality for the guardians: salt in a sensible range, peppers under brine, clean tools, patience, and honesty when a batch smells wrong.

**Craft is welcome. Improvisation on shelf-stability is not.**

This is the one masterclass lesson where grower humor stops at the kitchen door. Fermenting peppers for flavor is a craft. Claiming a bottle is **shelf-stable**, water-bath canning a low-acid or poorly acidified sauce, or inventing your own "safe pH" from a forum post is how people get hurt. Botulism risk in improperly preserved low-acid foods is rare in well-run kitchens and unacceptable to gamble with. If you cannot measure, follow a tested recipe, or keep the product refrigerated and eat it soon — do that. Heat does not sterilize a jar. Capsaicin is not a preservative.

**Two kitchens, do not cross the wires.**

IC SPICY CookBook ferments (LAB serum, BRV, FPJ) are **garden inputs** — diluted hard, sprayed or drenched on plants and soil. They teach fermentation *discipline* (clean vessels, smell cues, dilution respect). They are not permission to pour field concentrates into dinner, and they are not a canning SOP.

**Food-safety rail (unambiguous):**
1. **Refrigerated short ferments / fridge sauces** — lower archival risk; still use clean fruit, clean tools, and trusted salt/acid practice. When in doubt, compost the batch.
2. **Anything you call shelf-stable** — follow current FDA / USDA / National Center for Home Food Preservation / UF-IFAS guidance for *that exact process*. Measure pH with a calibrated meter if the process requires it. Do not freestyle.
3. **pH ~4.6** appears in many acidified-food / botulism-risk discussions as a critical threshold for certain preserved foods — treat it as a **[VERIFY]** checkpoint against current official docs for your method. For a **shelf-stable margin**, many tested acidified-food practices aim closer to **pH 4.2** (or lower) rather than riding the 4.6 line — still **[VERIFY]** against the exact process you are following. Ferment ≠ hot-fill acidified sauce ≠ water-bath canning ≠ pressure canning. Wrong process, wrong rule.
4. **Selling or gifting preserved foods** may trigger cottage-food or manufactured-food rules in Florida (DBPR / FDACS). Check before you label a bottle for someone else.
5. If you want commercial-grade certainty, consult a tested recipe source or a licensed cannery / process authority — not a spicy meme account.

Start with sound fruit from Lesson 6.1. Balance from 6.2 still applies after the ferment — acid will rise; you may still adjust salt, sweetness, or blending peppers before you call a *refrigerated* bottle finished.

## 🌶️ Deeper Heat

### What "sour guardians" are doing

Lactic acid bacteria convert sugars to lactic acid (and related metabolites). pH drops; competing organisms lose the neighborhood. Salt selects for salt-tolerant lactic organisms and draws juices to make brine. Oxygen management (weights keeping solids under brine) reduces surface yeast/mold drama.

This is ecology in a jar — same humility as soil biology, different habitat.

### Garden LAB vs food ferment (do not conflate)

CookBook LAB is rice-wash (optional milk) serum stabilized with sugar for **field** use at extreme dilution. [VERIFY vs CookBook: lactic-acid-bacteria-lab] Food lacto-ferments are salted vegetable packs. Sharing the word "lactic" does not make the recipes interchangeable.

BRV is a slow dual-stage vinegar for KNF extraction and spray acidification. [VERIFY vs CookBook: brown-rice-vinegar-brv] Buying food-grade vinegar for kitchen acidification is normal; your barrel of garden BRV is not automatically a sandwich vinegar unless you produced it to food standards on purpose.

### pH and process honesty

| Path | Responsible default |
|---|---|
| Short fridge ferment, eat soon | Clean practice; refrigerate; do not call it shelf-stable |
| Ambient ferment → fridge | Weights under brine; watch mold; skim only if a trusted tested recipe says so — when in doubt, compost |
| Hot sauce you want on a pantry shelf | Tested acidification + process from NCHFP / extension / process authority; calibrated pH meter; no improvisation |
| "I heard 4.6 somewhere" | [VERIFY] against current FDA/extension docs for the *exact* process — do not freestyle botulism math. Shelf-stable margin often targets **pH 4.2** (or lower), not the 4.6 cliff edge |
| "It's so hot it must be safe" | False. Capsaicin ≠ preservative. Discard questionable jars. |

IC SPICY will not publish a cowboy canning SOP in this lesson. We teach craft flavor and garden-ferment discipline. **Shelf-stability belongs to tested public methods** — or to refrigeration and short storage. Buy a pH meter before you claim a pantry bottle; when the stakes are commercial or unclear, ask a process authority / licensed cannery.

### Variety notes

- **Habanero (10)** — classic ferment aromatic; forgiving teaching fruit.
- **Scotch Bonnet (1)** — dense; only sound pods; dose heat with respect when blending post-ferment.

### What this is *not*

This is not a commercial processing license. Selling preserved foods may require cottage-food or manufactured-food compliance in your state — check Florida DBPR / FDACS rules separately.
