# 1.4 Feeding the Biology

**Outcome:** By the end of this lesson you can pick a carbon strategy that pays the microbial workforce — mulch, compost paths, and restrained sugar drenches — instead of leaving Florida beds bare and wondering why the peppers look broke.

---

## Core

Lesson 1.1 hired the workforce. This lesson is payroll.

Microbes do not eat fertilizer labels. They eat **carbon** — residues, mulches, composts, root exudates — and they need moisture and air like any crew on a hot shift. Leave a bed bare in Zone 10a July and you did not "keep it clean." You canceled direct deposit. UV, heat, and oxidation burn through what little organic matter sand was clinging to, and the plant inherits a hungry root zone with great drainage and nothing to do.

Payroll comes in envelopes of different sizes. Woody mulch is the slow paycheck that builds fungal housing. Thermophilic windrows are the hot kitchen that sanitizes and stabilizes bulk residues. Bokashi is the pre-compost punch card for kitchen scraps that would otherwise rot wrong. A Johnson-Su bioreactor is the long-game fungal-forward compost for growers who can wait. A molasses–microbe drench is a **bonus**, not a salary — a restrained sugar tip to wake existing biology, not a license to flood sand with syrup.

You do not need a compost theme park on day one. You need a habit: something living or once-living covering the soil, plus one bulk path that matches how much labor you actually have on a Tuesday. Soft greens and manures clear fast; wood chips clear slow — that C:N timing is why "just mix chips into the root zone" can steal nitrogen from seedlings while the same chips on top as mulch quietly build the fungal desk. Module 2's bottles (FPJ, JMS, and friends) work better when the crew still has that desk. Module 3 will turn payroll into bed architecture. Tonight is the decision to stop starving the people who feed the plant.

**One practical takeaway for tonight:** if your "feeding program" is only bottles and never carbon on the surface, you are tipping ghosts. Put mulch or compost on the bed before you argue about which tonic is elite.

## 🌶️ Deeper Heat

### SOM — the bank account (Florida honesty)

Soil organic matter (SOM) is the carbon bank that funds the workforce. Productive vegetable soils are often discussed in the ballpark of several percent SOM by weight; many Florida sands start much lower and lose what little they have when left bare. [VERIFY] Exact nursery targets belong on *your* soil tests and county guidance — not as a fake universal number in this lesson. Directionally: **build slow, destroy fast.** Tillage and bare soil are withdrawal fees.

### C:N — which paycheck clears when

Carbon-to-nitrogen ratio of residues controls whether microbes temporarily **tie up** nitrogen or **release** it while they work:

| Feedstock vibe | C:N tendency | Payroll behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh manure / fishy / soft greens | Lower C:N | Faster mineralization — quick cash, easy to overdo |
| Leaf litter / composted fines | Mid | Balanced cycling for beds |
| Wood chips / high-carbon mulch | Higher C:N | Slow fungal build; may immobilize N at the mulch–soil interface if mixed raw into the root zone |

Treat this table as grower orientation, not a certified lab sheet. Specific C:N numbers for your chip pile belong on a test or a feedstock card. [VERIFY] Operational rule for peppers: **mulch on top** for woody material; incorporate only what is finished enough not to steal nitrogen from seedlings.

### Five CookBook payroll tools

1. **Fungal-dominant mulch** — partially composted wood chips + IMO dusting; the default surface paycheck for pepper rows: [/cookbook/fungal-dominant-mulch](/cookbook/fungal-dominant-mulch)
2. **Molasses–microbe field drench** — restrained unsulfured molasses + indigenous biology; tip the existing crew, do not drown sand in sugar: [/cookbook/molasses-microbe-field-drench](/cookbook/molasses-microbe-field-drench)
3. **Johnson-Su bioreactor** — passive, long, fungal-forward compost for mycorrhizae-leaning finished material: [/cookbook/johnson-su-bioreactor](/cookbook/johnson-su-bioreactor)
4. **Bokashi pre-compost** — ferment kitchen/farm scraps before they hit a pile wrong: [/cookbook/bokashi-pre-compost](/cookbook/bokashi-pre-compost)
5. **Thermophilic windrow** — temperature/oxygen/moisture gates for poultry litter + bedding + residues so you do not ammonia-burn peppers later: [/cookbook/thermophilic-windrow](/cookbook/thermophilic-windrow)

You do not need all five running tomorrow. You need **one surface carbon habit** (mulch) and **one bulk compost path** that matches your labor. Module 3 turns these into bed-building SOPs.

### Sugar without the sugar rush

Molasses drenches are famous on the internet and infamous in sand when overdone. IC SPICY's CookBook framing is deliberate: feed **existing** microbes, sparingly. If the bed has no carbon housing and no inoculum history, syrup is a rave with no venue — a short bloom, then a crash. Pair restrained drenches with mulch and compost, not instead of them.

### Bridge to Module 3

Feeding biology is how you stop thinking in "bags per plant" and start thinking in **systems**: no-till principles, IMO culturing, compost/vermicompost, mulch and cover. This lesson closes Soil Steward theory. The next module puts shovels and rice boxes in your hands.

### What this is *not*

Payroll is not "never use a liquid input." FPJ, LAB, JMS, and friends (Module 2) are tools. They work best when the workforce still has a desk — organic matter, cover, and air.
