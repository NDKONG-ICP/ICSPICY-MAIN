# 1.2 Fungi & Mycorrhizae

**Outcome:** By the end of this lesson you can explain — in plain grower English — why chilies want a fungal-leaning root zone, what mycorrhizae actually trade for, and which nursery habits quietly fire that partnership.

---

## Core

Bacteria are the short-order cooks. Fungi are the fiber-optic network under the bed.

That is the one picture to keep. Mycelium threads through soil like an underground internet — thin cables that move water, phosphorus, and gossip between root neighborhoods. Your chili plant plugs into that network with **arbuscular mycorrhizae**: fungal partners that trade soil minerals and drought insurance for plant sugars. Nuke the cables (tillage, bare sand, a phosphorus dump) and the plant is stuck dialing nutrients on a landline root system that never left the house.

Peppers (*Capsicum*) are not forest trees, but they are not hydroponic lettuce either. Disturbed, high-nitrogen, constantly tilled beds skew bacterial. Leaf litter, woody mulch, and undisturbed structure skew fungal. Fruiting chilies — especially long-season superhots that sit in the same bed for months — do better when the fungal side of that spectrum is awake. You do not need a lab ratio tattooed on your forearm to act on that. You need cover, carbon, and inoculum that came from somewhere alive.

IC SPICY recruits that network the slow way and the fast way. The slow way is **fungal-dominant mulch** and charged biochar — housing and food for hyphae. The fast way is capturing local microbes (IMO Stage 1 rice boxes under leaf litter) and finishing them into IMO4 you can actually put in a bed. Compost tea is the courier service: biology in a bucket, used fresh. A Ghost or Habanero that will live in one row from cool season into humid summer is exactly the plant that notices when that network goes dark.

You will still use bacteria — Lesson 1.1's grazers still matter. The chili-specific upgrade is refusing to farm as if fungi were optional decoration. If the only "biology" in your program is a bottle with a cartoon microbe and a rototiller appointment every spring, you are paying for Wi-Fi and then cutting the cable.

**One practical takeaway for tonight:** if your Reaper looks "fed" on paper but sulks in July heat, ask whether the underground internet is still plugged in — or whether you rototilled the router last spring.

## 🌶️ Deeper Heat

### F:B balance without fake precision

Soil ecology talks about a fungal-to-bacterial (F:B) spectrum. Bare Florida sand after a till pass leans bacterial. A shaded leaf-mold understory leans fungal. Most annual vegetables sit somewhere in the middle; woody perennials and long-season fruiting crops often prefer more fungal influence over a season. Exact F:B target numbers are site- and method-dependent — do not treat a single ratio from a slide deck as gospel for Zone 10a sand. [VERIFY] If you ever cite a numeric F:B target for your nursery, pin it to a lab method and a soil type, not a vibe.

What you *can* manage without a microscope:

| Signal | More bacterial lean | More fungal lean |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | Bare, frequent till | Mulch, no-till, leaf litter |
| Carbon | Soft greens, sugars only | Woody chips, leaf mold, IMO solids |
| Disturbance | Rototill, constant foot traffic | Paths fixed, beds undisturbed |
| Soluble P | Heavy starter salts | Biology-mediated P via mycorrhizae |

### Mycorrhizae — the trade, not the magic

Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) colonize pepper roots and extend hyphae into soil pores roots cannot reach. Grower education often cites large surface-area multipliers for that hyphal network versus root surface alone; published estimates vary widely by species, soil, and measurement method. [VERIFY] Treat "multiplies root reach" as the operational truth; treat any specific "X times surface area" claim as something to confirm before you print it on a hangtag.

What reliably breaks the partnership:

1. **Tillage** — shears hyphae; resets colonization.
2. **Excess soluble phosphorus** — plant stops paying the fungal rent when P is cheap in the soil solution.
3. **Long bare fallows** — nothing to host or feed the network.
4. **Sterile media forever** — fine for plugs; a dead end if you never inoculate at transplant.

### IMO as local network install

KNF's IMO sequence is IC SPICY's way to **recruit local decomposers and associates** from habitats that already have the balance you want — not a catalog powder with a cartoon mushroom on the bag.

- **IMO1** — steamed rice under leaf litter; ready when white hyphae dominate, commonly **3–5 days**. [VERIFY vs CookBook: imo-stage-1-rice-capture]
- **IMO2 liquid** applications commonly land around **1:500–1:1,000**. [VERIFY vs CookBook: imo-stage-2-liquid-culture]
- **IMO4** — finished soil amendment you actually blend into beds: [/cookbook/imo-stage-4-soil-amendment](/cookbook/imo-stage-4-soil-amendment)

Module 3 walks the full capture-to-field path. This lesson only needs the *why*: you are installing a local network, not buying a VPN from a bottle.

### Mulch, biochar, tea — three jobs

- **Fungal-dominant mulch** — woody, partially composted chips + IMO dusting; feeds fungi that partner with pepper roots: [/cookbook/fungal-dominant-mulch](/cookbook/fungal-dominant-mulch)
- **Biochar–IMO charge** — pores become microbial apartments once charged; empty char is just thirsty carbon: [/cookbook/biochar-imo-charge](/cookbook/biochar-imo-charge)
- **Aerated compost tea** — brew **24–36 h**, use within **4 h**, keep dissolved oxygen honest (**DO >6 mg/L**), drench on the order of **10–20 gal / 1000 sq ft**. [VERIFY vs CookBook: aerated-compost-tea]

Tea without oxygen is a swamp party. Tea sitting overnight on a hot bench is yesterday's workforce on strike.

### What this is *not*

This is not "bacteria bad, fungi good." You need both. Lesson 1.1's grazers still release plant-available nutrients when they eat bacterial biomass. The chili-specific point is: **do not farm as if fungi were optional decoration.**
